# Trends and forces: AI adoption in European industrial machinery

*Part 1 of 4. Companion to the benchmark findings (02), the reinvention
opportunity map (03) and the AX design specification (04).*

This section sets the context the benchmark is read against: the forces
shaping how European industrial machinery manufacturers adopt AI in their own
operations. It draws on the same public sources as the assessments, dated
where the source is dated. It is a desk synthesis, not a market forecast.

---

## 1. The sector sells AI faster than it runs on it

The strongest single pattern in the source base is a split between AI as
product and AI as internal practice. Every company assessed communicates
extensively about AI it places on the market, and far less about AI it uses in
its own operations. Siemens states the distinction itself, describing internal
use of its shopfloor assistant at the Electronics Factory Erlangen as valuable
precisely because "we at Siemens are not only developing these generative AI
solutions but also actively using and testing them internally" (Siemens blog,
March 2025). That framing, developing-and-also-using, is the exception that
reveals the norm: for most firms the developing is disclosed in detail and the
using is not.

This matters because the two are different capabilities. Selling an AI product
demonstrates engineering. Running on AI internally demonstrates that the firm
can change how its own people work, which is the harder and less visible task.
The benchmark measures the second.

## 2. Regulation has become a board-level and public-affairs question

The EU AI Act moved from prospective to operative during the assessment
window, with enforcement milestones through 2026. Its effect on the sector is
already visible at the top of the largest firm: Siemens' CEO argued publicly
during 2026 that the Act treats industrial AI like consumer AI and that this
would push AI investment away from Europe, with much of a stated one-billion-euro
industrial-AI commitment directed to the United States (reported April to May
2026). Whatever one makes of the position, it establishes that AI regulation is
now a strategic and public-affairs matter for European machinery firms, not only
a compliance detail.

The benchmark records the consequence rather than the lobbying: only two of
eight companies disclose explicit EU AI Act preparation as part of their AI
governance, and one of those, Krones, did so before the Act was in force. The
gap between the sector's public engagement with the regulation and its
disclosed internal readiness for it is itself a finding.

## 3. Skilled-labour scarcity is the stated driver of internal adoption

Where firms do describe internal AI use, the justification is consistently the
shortage of experienced people rather than cost alone. Siemens frames its
Erlangen shopfloor assistant around the loss of expert knowledge through
turnover: "skilled labor is in short supply, and high employee turnover leads
to the loss of valuable knowledge" (Siemens blog, March 2025). KION frames its
MERLIN maintenance tool around the difficulty of reading service reports
"written under high time pressure". TRUMPF's Head of AI ties AI to the need to
become "more agile and flexible" as an organisation.

The pattern suggests the sector's most credible internal AI use cases are
knowledge-access and knowledge-retention problems, not automation-for-headcount.
That shapes where reinvention value is most likely to be real, and it informs
the opportunity map in part 3.

## 4. Generative AI has crossed from pilot to production, unevenly

Across the window, generative AI in the sector moved from announcement to at
least some internal production. KION describes MERLIN as its "first productively
used generative AI tool". TRUMPF's Head of AI reports using an internal tool,
blueGPT, in daily work. GEA runs a company-wide internal assistant, BetterBot.
Siemens' Erlangen assistant is "already in successful use".

But the crossing is uneven and shallow. In every case the disclosed internal
deployment is a single named tool, typically a knowledge or documentation
assistant, rather than an agentic workflow acting across process steps. No
company in the set discloses a second internal generative-AI application in
production. The frontier is real but narrow.

## 5. Governance and deployment are advancing out of step

The sector is not moving as a bloc on AI maturity, and the clearest evidence is
that governance and deployment do not track together. Some firms have built
governance structures ahead of visible deployment: Krones discloses an Advisory
Council on AI Strategy and EU AI Act preparation while its flagship internal use
case remained a pilot; Sandvik has a Chief AI Officer and a dedicated AI centre
but names no internal deployment. Others have deployed ahead of disclosed
governance: KION runs a generative AI tool in production and discloses no AI
governance at all.

This decoupling is the sector's defining structural feature at present. It
means "AI maturity" cannot be read from any single signal, and it is the reason
the benchmark scores six dimensions rather than one.

## 6. Nobody is yet publishing what their internal AI is worth

The final force is an absence. Across eight companies and two full search
passes, not one publishes a quantified result from its own AI use. Firms
publish precise figures constantly, but the figures attach to customer
outcomes and product performance, not to internal adoption. The sector has
begun to run on AI and has not yet begun to measure and disclose what that is
worth to it.

This is treated as a trend rather than an incidental gap because it is
universal in the set and because it has a clear direction of travel: the first
firms to publish credible internal outcome figures will do so into an empty
field. That opportunity is developed in parts 2 and 3.

## Sources and limitation

Every force above is grounded in the same public disclosure used for the
assessments; company-specific claims are cited to the source and date recorded
in the relevant file under `data/assessments/`. Regulatory and macro references
(EU AI Act timeline, executive statements on regulation) are drawn from dated
public reporting during the assessment window.

This is a desk synthesis by a single author. It describes what the sector
*discloses*, and the recurring theme, that disclosure of internal practice lags
disclosure of product, means the true state of internal adoption is very likely
ahead of what any outside assessment can see. The forces are read accordingly:
they describe the shape of the visible evidence, not a claim about what happens
inside these companies.
